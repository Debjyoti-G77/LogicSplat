================================================================================
                         DETAILED LITERATURE REVIEW
================================================================================

         LogicSplat: 3D Scene Graph Generation from Smartphone Video
         Using Gaussian Splat Geometry and Graph Neural Networks

================================================================================

4.1 Problem Context
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The generation of structured 3D scene graphs from visual input is a problem
that spans multiple research communities: computer vision, robotics, and
spatial computing. A scene graph is a directed graph where nodes represent
objects in a scene and edges represent semantic or spatial relationships
between them. The utility of scene graphs lies in their ability to compress
complex 3D environments into compact, queryable representations that support
downstream reasoning tasks such as robot manipulation planning, visual
question answering, and augmented reality interaction.

The specific challenge addressed by LogicSplat sits at the convergence of
three technical threads: (1) the emergence of 3D Gaussian Splatting as a
fast, high-quality scene representation from casual video, (2) the maturation
of graph neural networks for relational reasoning over structured data, and
(3) the growing interest in neuro-symbolic approaches that combine learned
perception with rule-based reasoning for robust spatial understanding.

This literature review examines 15 papers that collectively define the
landscape in which LogicSplat operates. Each paper is reviewed not merely for
its technical contribution but specifically for how it relates to, informs,
or contrasts with the approach taken in this project.

4.2 Research Questions Guiding the Review
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

RQ1: How do existing methods transition from raw 3D representations (point
     clouds, meshes, Gaussian splats) to structured object-level scene graphs?

RQ2: What role do foundation models (CLIP, SAM, GPT-4V) play in current
     state-of-the-art systems, and what performance is achievable without them?

RQ3: How is spatial relation prediction evaluated in the literature, and what
     protocols exist for separating object detection quality from relation
     prediction quality?

RQ4: What is known about domain transfer in 3D scene understanding — 
     specifically, do models trained on one type of 3D data generalise to
     another?

RQ5: What clustering or segmentation methods are used to decompose continuous
     3D representations into discrete objects, and what are their failure modes?

4.3 Review of Related Work
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Paper 1: 3D Gaussian Splatting for Real-Time Radiance Field Rendering
         Kerbl, B., Kopanas, G., Leimkuhler, T., and Drettakis, G.
         ACM Transactions on Graphics (SIGGRAPH), 2023
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

This paper introduced 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) as a fundamentally new
approach to novel view synthesis. Prior to this work, Neural Radiance Fields
(NeRF) dominated the field of neural scene representation, using implicit
multilayer perceptrons to map 3D coordinates to colour and density. While
NeRF produces high-quality renderings, it requires expensive per-ray network
evaluation that precludes real-time performance.

Kerbl et al. proposed representing scenes as collections of millions of
anisotropic 3D Gaussians, each parameterised by a mean position (3D), a full
3x3 covariance matrix (encoding orientation and scale), an opacity scalar,
and spherical harmonic coefficients for view-dependent colour. The key
technical innovation is a differentiable tile-based rasteriser that projects
these Gaussians onto the image plane and alpha-composites them in depth order,
achieving real-time rendering (>100 FPS) at quality competitive with or
exceeding NeRF.

For LogicSplat, this paper is foundational in two respects. First, the
explicit nature of the Gaussian representation (as opposed to implicit NeRF)
makes it possible to directly access per-primitive geometric properties
without neural network evaluation. Each Gaussian's covariance matrix encodes
the local shape of the surface it represents — flat surfaces produce
Gaussians with one small eigenvalue (planar), elongated structures produce
Gaussians with one large eigenvalue (linear), and compact objects produce
roughly spherical Gaussians. LogicSplat exploits these eigenvalue-derived
shape descriptors (planarity, elongation, sphericity) as physically meaningful
features for relation prediction.

Second, the speed of 3DGS reconstruction (minutes rather than hours for NeRF)
makes it practical to process casual smartphone video into a 3D representation
suitable for scene understanding. This accessibility is central to LogicSplat's
design philosophy of operating on consumer hardware with consumer input.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Paper 2: Learning 3D Semantic Scene Graphs from 3D Indoor Reconstructions
         Wald, J., Dhamo, H., Navab, N., and Tombari, F.
         CVPR, 2020
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

This paper established the foundational framework for 3D scene graph
prediction from point clouds. Wald et al. introduced the 3DSSG dataset,
containing semantically rich scene graphs for 3RScan indoor scenes with 160
object classes and 26 relationship types (including spatial relations like
"standing on," "lying on," "hanging on," and proximity relations like "close
by" and "same as").

The proposed architecture uses PointNet for per-object feature extraction from
point cloud segments, followed by a Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) that
propagates information between object nodes to predict pairwise relationships.
Critically, the evaluation protocol assumes ground truth instance segmentation
as input — the relation prediction module receives correctly segmented objects
and must only predict the relationships between them.

This evaluation design decision is directly relevant to LogicSplat. Our
project faces the same fundamental question: should relation prediction be
evaluated assuming correct object segmentation (isolating the relation
predictor's quality) or end-to-end including segmentation errors? Following
Wald et al.'s precedent, LogicSplat reports both: a "relation-only" metric
using oracle clustering, and an "end-to-end" metric with fully automatic
HDBSCAN clustering. The gap between these two metrics quantifies how much
performance is lost to clustering errors versus relation prediction errors.

The 3DSSG dataset also informed our relation vocabulary design. While 3DSSG
uses 26 relation types, many are semantically specific ("same material,"
"same shape") rather than spatially grounded. LogicSplat uses a reduced set
of 12 purely spatial relations that are derivable from 3D geometry alone,
reflecting our foundation-model-free constraint.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Paper 3: SceneGraphFusion: Incremental 3D Scene Graph Prediction from RGB-D
         Sequences
         Wu, S.C., Wald, J., Tateno, K., Navab, N., and Tombari, F.
         CVPR, 2021
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

SceneGraphFusion extends 3D scene graph prediction to an incremental setting,
building the graph progressively as new RGB-D frames arrive. The system
maintains a growing 3D instance map and updates the scene graph as new objects
are detected and new spatial relationships become observable.

The paper's most relevant contribution to LogicSplat is its evaluation
methodology. Wu et al. explicitly report results under two conditions: "given
ground truth instances" and "with geometric segments." The gap between these
two conditions is presented as a meaningful finding — it quantifies how much
of the end-to-end error is attributable to segmentation versus relation
prediction. This dual-reporting approach directly informed LogicSplat's
evaluation protocol design.

Additionally, SceneGraphFusion demonstrates that GNN-based relation prediction
can operate effectively on incrementally constructed, potentially noisy object
representations. This is encouraging for LogicSplat, where object
representations come from HDBSCAN clustering of Gaussian splats — a process
that introduces its own noise characteristics (centroid drift, bounding box
expansion, occasional object merging).

The key difference from LogicSplat is the input modality. SceneGraphFusion
uses RGB-D sensors with known metric depth, providing clean geometric
measurements. LogicSplat derives all 3D structure from monocular video via
Gaussian Splatting, introducing an additional layer of reconstruction noise
that compounds with clustering noise.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Paper 4: ReLaGS: Relational Language Gaussian Splatting
         Arafa, A. et al.
         CVPR, 2026
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

ReLaGS is the most directly comparable published work to LogicSplat. It
constructs hierarchical language-distilled Gaussian scenes and 3D semantic
scene graphs without scene-specific training. The system operates by: (1)
distilling CLIP features into individual Gaussians during reconstruction,
(2) performing Gaussian pruning to refine scene geometry, (3) aggregating
noisy 2D language features into accurate 3D object embeddings through
multi-view alignment, and (4) using a GNN to predict inter-object
relationships.

ReLaGS achieves unified open-vocabulary 3D perception — it can segment,
retrieve, and reason about objects using natural language queries without
any scene-specific fine-tuning. This represents the current state-of-the-art
for Gaussian-splat-based scene graph generation.

The fundamental difference from LogicSplat is the reliance on CLIP, a
foundation model requiring substantial GPU memory (several gigabytes for the
vision encoder alone) and compute. LogicSplat deliberately avoids all
foundation models, operating entirely on geometric and physical properties
extractable from the raw Gaussian parameters. This makes LogicSplat
significantly more lightweight and accessible but limits its semantic
understanding — it can determine that "object A is on top of object B" but
cannot determine that "the cup is on top of the book" without an additional
labeling step.

The existence of ReLaGS as a strong foundation-model-based competitor
positions LogicSplat's contribution differently: rather than claiming to
outperform ReLaGS, LogicSplat characterises what is achievable without
foundation models and identifies where the performance gap lies. This is a
legitimate and useful scientific contribution — understanding the value added
by foundation models requires understanding the baseline achievable without
them.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Paper 5: GaussianGraph: 3D Gaussian-based Scene Graph Generation for
         Open-world Scene Understanding
         Li, X. et al.
         arXiv:2503.04034, 2025
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

GaussianGraph addresses the same problem as ReLaGS but with a different
architectural approach. It uses adaptive semantic clustering on 3D Gaussians
(informed by 2D foundation model features projected into 3D) combined with a
"3D correction module" that refines initial relation predictions using
geometric constraints.

The 3D correction module is conceptually related to LogicSplat's geometry
rules. Both systems recognise that pure learned predictions can violate
physical constraints (e.g., predicting "on top of" for objects that are
horizontally separated) and apply geometric post-processing to enforce
consistency. However, GaussianGraph applies corrections as a refinement to
learned predictions, while LogicSplat uses geometry rules as the primary
prediction mechanism (with the GNN as an alternative that currently
underperforms the rules on real scenes).

GaussianGraph's reliance on 2D foundation models for clustering again
distinguishes it from LogicSplat's foundation-model-free approach. The
adaptive semantic clustering in GaussianGraph leverages SAM-style features
to determine object boundaries, while LogicSplat relies on HDBSCAN operating
on position and colour features alone. This difference is directly reflected
in clustering quality: GaussianGraph achieves reliable object segmentation
while LogicSplat's HDBSCAN frequently over-segments without oracle guidance.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Paper 6: NerfStudio: A Modular Framework for Neural Radiance Field Development
         Tancik, M. et al.
         ACM SIGGRAPH, 2023
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

NerfStudio provides the reconstruction backbone used in LogicSplat. It is a
modular, extensible framework that supports multiple neural scene
representation methods including NeRF variants and 3D Gaussian Splatting
(via its "splatfacto" implementation). The framework handles the complete
pipeline from input images to trained scene representation: structure-from-
motion via COLMAP for camera pose estimation, data loading and augmentation,
model training with configurable hyperparameters, and export of trained
representations.

For LogicSplat, NerfStudio serves as a black-box reconstruction tool. Input
smartphone video frames are processed through COLMAP (for camera intrinsics
and extrinsics) and then through splatfacto (for Gaussian Splatting training,
typically 30,000 iterations). The output is a .ply file containing all trained
Gaussian parameters. LogicSplat operates entirely on this exported file and
does not interact with NerfStudio during inference.

The choice of NerfStudio over alternative reconstruction tools (e.g., the
original 3DGS implementation, InstantNGP, or commercial photogrammetry
software) was motivated by its ease of use, active maintenance, and the
quality of its splatfacto implementation for casual smartphone video input.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Paper 7: HDBSCAN: Hierarchical Density-Based Spatial Clustering of
         Applications with Noise
         McInnes, L., Healy, J., and Astels, S.
         Journal of Open Source Software, 2017
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

HDBSCAN is the clustering algorithm used in LogicSplat to segment the
Gaussian splat into individual objects. It extends DBSCAN by performing
clustering over varying density thresholds and selecting the most stable
clusters across the hierarchy. Unlike DBSCAN, HDBSCAN does not require a
fixed epsilon (neighbourhood radius) parameter, making it more suitable for
data with clusters of varying densities.

The algorithm's key parameter is min_cluster_size, which sets the minimum
number of points required to form a cluster. For Gaussian splats, this
parameter must be tuned to the expected object size: too small and noise
fragments become clusters, too large and small objects are absorbed into
larger ones. LogicSplat implements an auto-tuning mechanism that sweeps
min_cluster_size values and selects the configuration that produces a cluster
count closest to an expected target (when available).

The primary limitation of HDBSCAN for this application is its tendency to
over-segment when the density contrast between objects is low. In tabletop
scenes where objects are close together and the Gaussian splat has colour
bleeding at boundaries, HDBSCAN frequently produces 7-8 clusters for scenes
containing 4-5 objects. This over-segmentation is currently the primary
bottleneck for LogicSplat's end-to-end performance, as documented in the
results chapter.

Paper 8: Graph Attention Networks
         Velickovic, P., Cucurull, G., Casanova, A., Romero, A., Lio, P.,
         and Bengio, Y.
         ICLR, 2018
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Graph Attention Networks (GATs) introduce masked self-attention mechanisms
into graph neural networks, allowing each node to attend to its neighbours
with learned importance weights rather than treating all neighbours equally
(as in GCN) or using fixed aggregation (as in GraphSAGE). The multi-head
attention mechanism computes K independent attention functions and
concatenates or averages their outputs, providing both expressiveness and
training stability.

LogicSplat uses a 2-layer GAT with 4 attention heads (averaged, not
concatenated) as the backbone of its learned relation predictor. The
attention mechanism is particularly relevant for scene graphs because not
all spatial neighbours are equally informative for predicting a given
relation. For example, when predicting whether object A is "on top of"
object B, the objects directly above and below A are more informative than
objects far to the side. The attention mechanism can learn to weight these
informative neighbours more heavily.

The GAT backbone feeds into a dual-head edge classifier: one head specialised
for directional relations (left_of, right_of, in_front_of, behind,
higher_than, lower_than) and another for contact/physical relations
(on_top_of, under, inside, attached_to, adjacent_to, hanging_from). This
dual-head design was motivated by the finding that these two relation
categories require different feature sensitivities and suffer from gradient
interference when sharing a single classifier.

Paper 9: ScanNet: Richly-annotated 3D Reconstructions of Indoor Scenes
         Dai, A., Chang, A.X., Savva, M., Halber, M., Funkhouser, T.,
         and Niessner, M.
         CVPR, 2017
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

ScanNet is a large-scale dataset of 1513 RGB-D scans of indoor environments
with dense semantic annotations. Each scan provides a reconstructed triangle
mesh, per-vertex NYU40 semantic labels, per-vertex instance segment IDs, and
object aggregation groupings that map segments to coherent objects. The
dataset also provides axis-alignment matrices that rotate each scan to a
canonical gravity-aligned orientation.

LogicSplat uses ScanNet as its primary training data source. Per-object
geometry is extracted from the mesh vertices (grouped by aggregation), and
spatial relations are derived from 3D bounding box geometry using rule-based
logic. The axis-alignment matrices are applied to ensure consistent
orientation across scenes, making directional relations (in_front_of, behind)
learnable.

A critical finding of this project is that models trained on ScanNet's
room-scale geometry (scenes spanning 3-8 metres with dozens of objects) do
not transfer well to tabletop-scale scenes (spanning 0.3-0.8 metres with 3-6
objects). Despite feature normalisation, the learned decision boundaries are
calibrated for room-scale distributions and produce systematic errors at
tabletop scale. This domain gap (ScanNet test F1 = 0.894 versus tabletop
deployment F1 = 0.322) is one of the primary findings reported in this work.

Paper 10: Asymmetric Loss for Multi-Label Classification
          Ridnik, T., Ben-Baruch, E., Zamir, N., Noy, A., Friedman, I.,
          Protter, M., and Zelnik-Manor, L.
          ICCV, 2021
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

This paper addresses the challenge of training multi-label classifiers when
positive examples are rare relative to negatives. In standard binary cross-
entropy, the gradient contribution from the overwhelming number of easy
negatives dominates training, preventing the model from learning to detect
rare positive classes effectively. Focal loss (Lin et al., 2017) partially
addresses this by down-weighting easy examples, but treats positives and
negatives symmetrically.

Ridnik et al. propose Asymmetric Loss (ASL), which applies different focusing
parameters to positive and negative examples. Specifically, it uses a higher
gamma for negatives (aggressively down-weighting easy negatives) and a lower
gamma for positives (preserving gradient signal from all positive examples).
This asymmetry is particularly effective for multi-label problems where each
class has its own positive/negative ratio.

LogicSplat adopts this approach for its contact relation classes. In the
training data, on_top_of has approximately 4,200 positive edges versus
560,000 total edges (a 0.75% positive rate), while left_of has 67,000
positives (12% positive rate). Standard BCE with pos_weight partially
compensates but does not address the hard-negative problem: the most
confusing negatives for on_top_of are pairs that are higher_than but not
in contact. Asymmetric focal loss (gamma_neg=2.0, gamma_pos=0.0) applied
selectively to contact relations improved on_top_of F1 from 0.598 to 0.721
(before threshold tuning) on ScanNet test data.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Paper 11: OCTScenes: A Versatile Real-World Dataset of Tabletop Scenes for
          Object-Centric Learning
          Reza, S. et al.
          NeurIPS 2024 Datasets and Benchmarks Track
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

OCTScenes provides 5000 real-world tabletop scenes captured with consumer
cameras at 640x480 resolution, with 60 frames per scene providing multi-view
coverage. The dataset is designed for object-centric learning tasks including
object discovery, segmentation, and novel view synthesis. Each scene contains
3-8 common household objects arranged on a table surface.

LogicSplat uses a subset of 19 OCTScenes as additional evaluation data. The
scenes were downloaded, extracted, and processed through NerfStudio to produce
Gaussian splats. However, the dataset does not provide spatial relation
annotations — these must be generated manually. The annotation process is
currently blocked by the difficulty of matching HDBSCAN clusters to physical
objects without a dedicated cluster visualisation tool.

The value of OCTScenes for LogicSplat lies in its scale (19 additional test
scenes would bring the total to 27, substantially improving statistical
power) and its domain match (real tabletop scenes captured with consumer
cameras, matching LogicSplat's target deployment scenario). Completing the
annotation of these scenes is a priority for the next phase of the project.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Paper 12: Gaussian Grouping: Segment and Edit Anything in 3D Scenes
          Ye, M., Danelljan, M., Yu, F., and Ke, L.
          ECCV, 2024
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Gaussian Grouping extends 3D Gaussian Splatting to jointly reconstruct and
segment objects in open-world 3D scenes. Each Gaussian is augmented with a
compact identity encoding that allows Gaussians to be grouped according to
their object instance membership. The identity encodings are supervised using
2D segmentation masks from SAM (Segment Anything Model) projected across
multiple views.

This paper is relevant to LogicSplat primarily as a potential solution to the
clustering bottleneck. LogicSplat currently uses HDBSCAN on position and
colour features, which frequently over-segments without oracle guidance.
Gaussian Grouping achieves significantly more reliable object segmentation by
leveraging SAM's 2D segmentation capability projected into 3D. However,
adopting Gaussian Grouping would require SAM (a foundation model), which
contradicts LogicSplat's design philosophy.

The tension between LogicSplat's foundation-model-free constraint and the
superior segmentation quality achievable with foundation-model-assisted
methods (Gaussian Grouping, SAGA) represents a fundamental design tradeoff.
The current project characterises this tradeoff quantitatively: the 3x
performance drop from oracle clustering to automatic HDBSCAN clustering
represents the cost of avoiding foundation models for segmentation.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Paper 13: SAGA: Segment Any 3D Gaussians
          Cen, J. et al.
          arXiv:2312.00860, 2023
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

SAGA (Segment Any 3D Gaussians) provides promptable 3D segmentation of
Gaussian splat scenes. Given 2D visual prompts (points, boxes, or masks),
SAGA can segment the corresponding 3D region represented by Gaussians within
4 milliseconds. The method trains a lightweight feature field alongside the
Gaussian splat that enables efficient 3D-consistent segmentation.

Like Gaussian Grouping, SAGA represents a potential upgrade path for
LogicSplat's clustering module. Its promptable nature is particularly
interesting: one could imagine a system where an initial coarse clustering
(HDBSCAN) provides prompts that SAGA refines into precise object boundaries.
However, SAGA's reliance on SAM features for training again introduces a
foundation model dependency.

For the purposes of this project, SAGA and Gaussian Grouping are noted as
the state-of-the-art for Gaussian segmentation and as the natural next step
if the foundation-model-free constraint is relaxed. They also serve as an
upper bound on what clustering quality is achievable, contextualising
LogicSplat's HDBSCAN results.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Paper 14: Neuro-Symbolic Spatial Reasoning in Segmentation
          Authors et al.
          arXiv:2510.15841, 2024
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

This paper introduces neuro-symbolic spatial reasoning into open-vocabulary
semantic segmentation. The key insight is that vision-language models suffer
from a lack of understanding of spatial relations between objects — they can
identify objects but cannot reliably determine their spatial arrangement. The
proposed solution integrates symbolic spatial reasoning rules with neural
perception, using logical constraints to improve segmentation consistency.

This work validates LogicSplat's neuro-symbolic approach from a different
angle. Where LogicSplat uses symbolic geometry rules to predict spatial
relations from 3D geometry, this paper uses symbolic spatial rules to improve
2D segmentation. Both demonstrate that explicit symbolic reasoning about
spatial relationships provides value that pure neural approaches miss.

The planned symbolic consistency repair module in LogicSplat (enforcing
constraints like "on_top_of implies higher_than" and "left_of conflicts with
right_of for the same pair") is directly inspired by this line of work. The
key difference is that LogicSplat operates in 3D with known geometry, making
the spatial constraints more precise and verifiable than in the 2D case.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Paper 15: Edge-Centric Relational Reasoning for 3D Scene Graph Prediction
          Authors et al.
          arXiv:2511.15288, 2024
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

This paper proposes an edge-centric approach to 3D scene graph prediction,
arguing that existing object-centric GNN architectures (where edge features
are updated by aggregating messages from connected nodes) are suboptimal for
relation prediction. Instead, the edge-centric approach treats relation edges
as first-class entities with their own representation learning pathway,
allowing direct edge-to-edge message passing.

This is relevant to LogicSplat's architectural decisions. The current v7
dual-head architecture uses a standard node-centric GAT backbone followed by
an edge classifier MLP. The edge features (17-dim geometric descriptors) are
concatenated with node embeddings but do not participate in the message
passing process. An edge-centric architecture could potentially improve
relation prediction by allowing edges to directly influence each other —
for example, if edge (A, B) predicts "on_top_of," this should inform edge
(B, A) to predict "under."

This represents a potential architectural improvement for future versions of
LogicSplat, particularly if the current architecture's performance ceiling
on contact relations proves insufficient after addressing the domain gap
through better training data.

4.5 Research Gap
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The literature review reveals a clear and specific gap: no existing published
work performs spatial relation prediction directly from Gaussian splat
geometric and physical properties without relying on foundation models for
semantic grounding or object segmentation.

The closest works (ReLaGS, GaussianGraph) achieve strong results but require
CLIP, SAM, or similar large pre-trained models that demand significant
computational resources. The question of whether meaningful spatial reasoning
can be extracted from Gaussian geometry alone — using only the positions,
covariances, opacities, and colours of the Gaussians themselves — remains
unanswered in the literature.

Furthermore, the domain transfer problem (training on room-scale data,
deploying on tabletop-scale data) is under-characterised. Most 3D scene graph
papers evaluate on the same distribution they train on (e.g., train on
ScanNet, test on ScanNet). The cross-domain evaluation that LogicSplat
performs (train on ScanNet, test on real Gaussian splat scenes) exposes a
failure mode that is not visible in same-distribution evaluation.

LogicSplat addresses both gaps: it characterises what is achievable without
foundation models, and it honestly reports the domain transfer challenge that
arises when moving from clean mesh-based training data to noisy Gaussian
splat deployment data. Both the capabilities and the limitations constitute
genuine contributions to the field's understanding.
