3. INTRODUCTION
================================================================================

3.1 Background and Motivation
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Three-dimensional scene understanding is a fundamental capability for robotics,
augmented reality, and spatial computing. A robot operating in a kitchen must
understand not merely that objects exist, but how they relate to one another
spatially: the cup is on top of the saucer, the knife is to the left of the
plate, the book is inside the drawer. This structured understanding is
typically represented as a scene graph, where nodes represent objects and edges
represent spatial or semantic relationships between them.

Recent advances in Neural Radiance Fields and, more recently, 3D Gaussian
Splatting (3DGS) have made it possible to reconstruct high-fidelity 3D scenes
from casual smartphone video in a matter of minutes. A user can walk around a
table, record a short video, and obtain a dense 3D representation of the scene
as a collection of millions of oriented Gaussian primitives, each carrying
position, covariance, opacity, and colour information.

However, a gap exists between 3D reconstruction and 3D understanding. The
Gaussian splat is a photorealistic representation optimised for rendering, not
for reasoning. Extracting a structured scene graph from this unstructured
collection of Gaussians requires solving several sub-problems: identifying
which Gaussians belong to which object (segmentation/clustering), determining
the spatial extent and properties of each object, and inferring the spatial
relationships between all pairs of objects.

The dominant approaches to 3D scene graph generation rely heavily on foundation
models. Systems such as ReLaGS (Arafa et al., 2025) use CLIP and SAM for
semantic grounding, while GaussianGraph (Li et al., 2024) employs 2D
foundation models for adaptive semantic clustering. These approaches achieve
strong results but require significant computational resources (large GPU
memory for foundation model inference) and depend on proprietary or
resource-intensive pre-trained models.

This project, LogicSplat, explores an alternative philosophy: can we build a
functional 3D scene graph system from consumer video that operates entirely
without foundation models, runs on consumer hardware (a single RTX GPU), and
relies instead on the geometric and physical properties inherent in the
Gaussian representation itself? The motivation is both practical (accessibility
for robotics researchers without access to large compute) and scientific
(understanding what information is actually encoded in Gaussian splat geometry
versus what requires external semantic knowledge).

3.2 Problem Statement
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Given a casually captured smartphone video of a tabletop scene, the system must
produce a structured 3D scene graph consisting of:
  (a) identified objects with spatial extent and optional semantic labels, and
  (b) directed spatial relations between all object pairs from a defined
      vocabulary of 12 relation types.

The system must operate without any foundation models (no CLIP, SAM, GPT-4V,
or similar), must run on consumer hardware, and must produce results that are
accurate enough to be useful for downstream robotic reasoning tasks.

3.3 Objectives
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The primary objectives of this project are:

  1. To develop a complete pipeline from smartphone video to 3D scene graph
     using only Gaussian splat geometry and learned lightweight models.

  2. To train a Graph Attention Network (GAT) for multi-label spatial relation
     prediction, initially on ScanNet room-scale data and subsequently
     fine-tuned on tabletop-scale data.

  3. To investigate whether Gaussian-specific physical features (covariance
     eigenvalues, opacity distributions, point density) provide discriminative
     signal for spatial relation prediction beyond what bounding-box geometry
     alone offers.

  4. To establish a rigorous evaluation protocol that separates clustering
     quality from relation prediction quality, following standards from the
     3D scene graph literature.

  5. To build an end-user application (Streamlit interface) that allows
     natural language queries about spatial arrangements in a scene.

3.4 Scope
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The current scope is limited to static tabletop scenes containing 3 to 8
rigid objects. The relation vocabulary consists of 12 spatial relations:
on_top_of, under, inside, attached_to, hanging_from, adjacent_to, left_of,
right_of, in_front_of, behind, higher_than, and lower_than. Dynamic scenes,
articulated objects, and outdoor environments are explicitly out of scope.

The system targets workshop-level publication at venues such as IROS, ICRA
robotics workshops, or 3DV workshops.

3.5 Significance of the Study
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

This work contributes to the field in several ways. First, it demonstrates
that meaningful spatial reasoning can be extracted from Gaussian splat
representations without relying on foundation models, establishing a baseline
for lightweight scene understanding. Second, it provides an honest
characterisation of the domain gap between room-scale training data (ScanNet)
and tabletop-scale deployment, a problem that is under-reported in the
literature. Third, it introduces a physically-motivated feature set derived
from Gaussian covariance eigenvalues (planarity, elongation, sphericity,
support likelihood) and demonstrates through SHAP analysis that these features
carry discriminative signal for contact relations. Fourth, it proposes a
rigorous evaluation protocol that separates clustering quality from relation
prediction quality, addressing a methodological gap in existing 3D scene graph
evaluation.

The remainder of this report is organised as follows. Chapter 4 reviews the

4. LITERATURE REVIEW
================================================================================

4.1 Problem Context
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The problem addressed by LogicSplat sits at the intersection of three active
research areas: 3D Gaussian Splatting for scene representation, 3D scene graph
generation for structured understanding, and graph neural networks for
relational reasoning. Each of these areas has seen rapid progress in 2023-2025,
and the relevant works are reviewed below with specific attention to how they
relate to and differ from the approach taken in this project.

4.2 Research Questions
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The literature review is guided by the following questions:

  RQ1: How do existing 3D scene graph methods handle the transition from raw
       3D representations to structured object-level graphs?

  RQ2: What role do foundation models play in current state-of-the-art
       systems, and what alternatives exist for resource-constrained settings?

  RQ3: How is spatial relation prediction evaluated in the literature, and
       what protocols exist for separating segmentation quality from relation
       prediction quality?

  RQ4: What is known about the domain gap between training data (typically
       room-scale indoor scans) and deployment scenarios (tabletop robotics)?

4.3 Review of Related Work
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Paper 1: 3D Gaussian Splatting for Real-Time Radiance Field Rendering
(Kerbl et al., 2023)

This foundational paper introduced 3D Gaussian Splatting as an alternative to
Neural Radiance Fields for novel view synthesis. The key innovation is
representing scenes as collections of anisotropic 3D Gaussians, each
parameterised by position, covariance matrix, opacity, and spherical harmonic
colour coefficients. The differentiable rasterisation pipeline enables
real-time rendering at high quality while maintaining an explicit, editable
scene representation. For LogicSplat, this work provides the underlying scene
representation. The explicit nature of Gaussians (as opposed to implicit NeRF
representations) is what makes direct geometric reasoning possible without
neural rendering at inference time. We exploit the per-Gaussian covariance
matrices as a source of physical shape information (planarity, elongation)
that is not available in mesh-based or point-cloud-based representations.

Paper 2: Learning 3D Semantic Scene Graphs from 3D Indoor Reconstructions
(Wald et al., 2020 — 3DSSG, CVPR 2020)

This paper introduced the 3DSSG dataset and a PointNet + GCN architecture for
predicting scene graphs from 3D point clouds. The dataset provides
semi-automatically generated scene graphs for 3RScan indoor scenes with 160
object classes and 26 relationship types. Critically, the evaluation protocol
uses ground truth instance segmentation as input to the relation prediction
module, establishing the precedent that relation prediction should be evaluated
separately from object detection. LogicSplat follows this evaluation philosophy
by reporting relation-only metrics conditioned on successful clustering, in
addition to end-to-end metrics. However, unlike 3DSSG which operates on
complete room-scale reconstructions with rich semantic annotations, LogicSplat
operates on noisy Gaussian splat clusters from casual phone video with no
semantic labels.

Paper 3: SceneGraphFusion: Incremental 3D Scene Graph Prediction from RGB-D
Sequences (Wu et al., 2021 — CVPR 2021)

SceneGraphFusion builds scene graphs incrementally from RGB-D video streams,
using a graph neural network that operates on incrementally constructed 3D
instance maps. The paper explicitly reports results under two conditions:
"given ground truth instances" and "with geometric segments," establishing
that the gap between these two conditions is a meaningful finding rather than
a limitation to hide. This directly informed our evaluation protocol design.
The key difference from LogicSplat is the input modality: SceneGraphFusion
uses RGB-D sensors with known depth, while LogicSplat derives 3D structure
entirely from monocular video via Gaussian Splatting, introducing additional
noise from the reconstruction process.

Paper 4: ReLaGS: Relational Language Gaussian Splatting
(Arafa et al., 2025 — CVPR 2026)

ReLaGS is the most directly comparable recent work. It constructs hierarchical
language-distilled Gaussian scenes and 3D semantic scene graphs without
scene-specific training. The system uses CLIP features distilled into Gaussians
for open-vocabulary object identification and a GNN for relation prediction.
It represents the current state-of-the-art for Gaussian-splat-based scene
graph generation. LogicSplat differs fundamentally in its design philosophy:
where ReLaGS relies on CLIP (a foundation model requiring significant GPU
memory and compute), LogicSplat operates entirely without foundation models.
This makes LogicSplat more accessible for consumer hardware but limits its
semantic understanding capabilities. ReLaGS achieves strong results on
ScanNet-style benchmarks; LogicSplat's contribution is demonstrating what is
achievable without such resources and characterising the resulting limitations
honestly.

Paper 5: GaussianGraph: 3D Gaussian-based Scene Graph Generation for
Open-world Scene Understanding (Li et al., 2025)

GaussianGraph uses adaptive semantic clustering on 3D Gaussians combined with
2D foundation model features and spatial correction modules for scene graph
generation. It introduces a "3D correction module" that refines initial
relation predictions using geometric constraints. This concept of geometric
correction is related to LogicSplat's geometry rules, though GaussianGraph
applies corrections as a post-processing refinement to learned predictions
rather than as a primary prediction mechanism. The reliance on 2D foundation
models for clustering again distinguishes it from LogicSplat's
foundation-model-free approach.

Paper 6: NerfStudio: A Modular Framework for Neural Radiance Field Development
(Tancik et al., 2023)

NerfStudio provides the reconstruction backbone used in LogicSplat. Its
splatfacto implementation of 3D Gaussian Splatting, combined with COLMAP for
structure-from-motion initialisation, enables the conversion of casual phone
video into Gaussian splat representations. The framework's modular design
allows straightforward extraction of the trained Gaussian parameters (position,
covariance, opacity, SH coefficients) for downstream processing. LogicSplat
uses NerfStudio as a black-box reconstruction tool and operates entirely on
the output .ply file.

Paper 7: HDBSCAN: Hierarchical Density-Based Spatial Clustering of
Applications with Noise (McInnes et al., 2017)

HDBSCAN is the clustering algorithm used in LogicSplat to segment the
Gaussian splat into individual objects. Unlike DBSCAN, HDBSCAN does not
require a fixed epsilon parameter and can discover clusters of varying
densities. The algorithm's stability-based cluster selection is particularly
relevant for Gaussian splats where object densities vary significantly (a
dense ceramic mug versus a sparse transparent glass). However, as our results
demonstrate, HDBSCAN's performance is highly sensitive to the min_cluster_size
parameter and tends to over-segment tabletop scenes without guidance on the
expected number of objects. This clustering instability is currently the
primary bottleneck for end-to-end system performance.

Paper 8: Graph Attention Networks (Velickovic et al., 2018 — ICLR 2018)

The Graph Attention Network (GAT) architecture forms the backbone of
LogicSplat's learned relation predictor. GATs introduce attention mechanisms
into graph neural networks, allowing nodes to attend to their neighbours with
learned importance weights. This is particularly relevant for scene graphs
where not all neighbouring objects are equally informative for predicting a
given relation. LogicSplat uses a 2-layer GAT with 4 attention heads, followed
by a dual-head edge classifier that separately processes directional relations
and contact/physical relations.

Paper 9: ScanNet: Richly-annotated 3D Reconstructions of Indoor Scenes
(Dai et al., 2017 — CVPR 2017)

ScanNet provides the primary training data for LogicSplat's GNN. The dataset
contains 1513 RGB-D scans of indoor scenes with dense semantic annotations,
instance segmentation, and axis-aligned bounding boxes. LogicSplat uses
ScanNet's per-object geometry (extracted from mesh vertices and aggregation
files) to train the relation prediction GNN. A critical finding of this
project is that models trained on ScanNet's room-scale geometry (scenes
spanning 3-8 metres) do not transfer well to tabletop-scale scenes (spanning
0.3-0.8 metres), despite normalisation efforts. This domain gap is quantified
in Chapter 6.

Paper 10: Asymmetric Loss for Multi-Label Classification
(Ridnik et al., 2021 — ICCV 2021)

This paper introduces asymmetric focal loss for multi-label classification
problems with severe class imbalance. The key insight is that in multi-label
settings, negative examples vastly outnumber positives for rare classes, and
standard focal loss treats positives and negatives symmetrically. Asymmetric
loss applies different focusing parameters to positive and negative examples,
effectively down-weighting easy negatives more aggressively. LogicSplat adopts
this approach for its contact relation classes (on_top_of, under, inside,
attached_to) which have 10-60x fewer positive samples than directional
relations (left_of, right_of).

Paper 11: OCTScenes: A Versatile Real-World Dataset of Tabletop Scenes for
Object-Centric Learning (Reza et al., 2024)

OCTScenes provides 5000 real-world tabletop scenes captured at 640x480
resolution with 60 frames per scene. The dataset is designed for object-centric
learning and provides multi-view coverage of tabletop arrangements. LogicSplat
uses a subset of 19 OCTScenes processed through NerfStudio as additional
evaluation data. The dataset's tabletop focus makes it directly relevant to
our deployment scenario, though the lack of spatial relation annotations
requires manual annotation effort that is currently incomplete.

Paper 12: SGFN: Semantic Scene Graph Generation from Point Clouds
(Wu et al., 2023)

SGFN extends scene graph prediction to operate directly on point clouds using
a semantic graph transformer architecture. The paper demonstrates that
point-level features can be aggregated into object-level representations
suitable for relation prediction without requiring explicit instance
segmentation as a preprocessing step. This is conceptually related to
LogicSplat's approach of extracting object features directly from Gaussian
clusters, though SGFN uses a transformer architecture rather than GAT and
operates on semantically labelled point clouds rather than raw Gaussians.

Paper 13: 3D Gaussian Splatting as a New Era: A Survey
(Fang et al., 2024 — IEEE TVCG)

This comprehensive survey covers the rapid development of 3D Gaussian
Splatting across reconstruction, rendering, editing, and understanding tasks.
It identifies scene understanding as an emerging application area for 3DGS
and notes that most existing approaches rely on distilling features from 2D
foundation models into the Gaussian representation. The survey does not
identify any existing work that performs spatial relation prediction directly
from Gaussian geometric properties without foundation model assistance,
suggesting that LogicSplat's approach occupies a genuine gap in the literature.

Paper 14: Contrastive Gaussian Clustering for Weakly Supervised 3D Scene
Segmentation (Yang et al., 2024)

This paper addresses the problem of segmenting 3D Gaussian scenes into
objects using contrastive learning on Gaussian features. The approach learns
to cluster Gaussians into semantically meaningful groups without requiring
dense per-Gaussian labels. This is directly relevant to LogicSplat's
clustering challenge: our current HDBSCAN approach operates on position and
colour features only, while contrastive clustering could potentially learn
more discriminative features for object separation. This represents a
potential improvement path for our clustering bottleneck.

Paper 15: Building Probabilistic Functional 3D Scene Graphs via Factor-Graph
Reasoning (Maggio et al., 2025)

This recent work introduces probabilistic reasoning over scene graphs using
factor graphs, enabling uncertainty-aware relation prediction and constraint
propagation. The approach is relevant to LogicSplat's planned symbolic
consistency repair module, which aims to enforce physical constraints
(e.g., on_top_of implies higher_than) as a post-processing step. The
factor-graph formulation provides a principled probabilistic framework for
such constraint enforcement, as opposed to the deterministic rule-based
approach currently planned for LogicSplat.

4.5 Research Gap
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The literature review reveals a clear gap: no existing published work performs
spatial relation prediction directly from Gaussian splat geometric and physical
properties without relying on foundation models for semantic grounding. ReLaGS
and GaussianGraph both achieve strong results but require CLIP, SAM, or similar
large pre-trained models. The question of whether meaningful spatial reasoning
can be extracted from Gaussian geometry alone — and what the limitations of
such an approach are — remains unanswered. LogicSplat addresses this gap
directly, and the honest characterisation of both capabilities and limitations
constitutes the primary contribution of this work.

5. METHODOLOGY
================================================================================

5.1 Overview
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

LogicSplat operates as a sequential pipeline with five major stages:
reconstruction, clustering, feature extraction, relation prediction, and
evaluation. A smartphone video of a tabletop scene (approximately 60 frames at
640x480 resolution) is first processed through NerfStudio's splatfacto
implementation to produce a 3D Gaussian Splat (.ply file containing 200,000 to
500,000 Gaussians). This splat is then clustered using HDBSCAN to identify
individual objects. Per-object and per-pair geometric features are extracted
from the cluster statistics. Finally, spatial relations are predicted using one
of several methods: hand-crafted geometry rules with dynamic thresholds, a
trained Graph Attention Network, per-relation logistic regression classifiers
with physical features, or an ensemble combining multiple approaches.

The system is implemented in Python using PyTorch, PyTorch Geometric, and
scikit-learn. All training and inference runs on a single NVIDIA RTX GPU.

5.2 Data Collection
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

5.2.1 Data Sources

Three data sources are used in this project:

ScanNet (Dai et al., 2017): 1513 indoor RGB-D scans with dense mesh
reconstructions, per-vertex semantic labels, instance segmentation, and
axis-alignment matrices. Used for pre-training the GNN. Located on local
storage (D:/scannet/scans). Each scene provides a triangular mesh (.ply),
per-vertex NYU40 label IDs, per-vertex segment IDs, and object aggregation
groupings (JSON). The axis-alignment matrix rotates each scan to a canonical
gravity-aligned orientation where Z is up and X/Y are consistent across scenes.

Custom Tabletop Scenes: 8 scenes (scene_06 through scene_13) recorded by the
author using a smartphone camera. Each scene contains 3-5 common tabletop
objects (router, box, bottle, cup, book, etc.) arranged on a desk surface.
Videos are approximately 60 frames captured while walking around the scene.
These scenes are processed through NerfStudio (COLMAP + splatfacto, 30,000
iterations) to produce Gaussian splats. Ground truth relations are manually
annotated and verified using a custom verification script.

OCTScenes (Reza et al., 2024): 19 scenes from the OCTScenes dataset (640x480,
60 frames each) downloaded and processed through NerfStudio. These provide
additional real-world tabletop evaluation data. Annotation is partially
complete (annotation templates generated with auto-derived relations, but
manual verification is blocked by difficulty in identifying which cluster
corresponds to which physical object in the scene).

5.2.2 Adopted Approaches

For ScanNet processing, per-object geometry is extracted by: (a) reading the
mesh vertices, (b) applying the axis-alignment matrix for canonical
orientation, (c) grouping vertices by segment ID and aggregation groupings,
(d) computing per-object bounding boxes and centroids, and (e) deriving
spatial relations from 3D bounding box geometry using rule-based logic.

For custom scenes, the pipeline is: (a) record video with smartphone,
(b) extract frames, (c) run NerfStudio (COLMAP for camera poses, splatfacto
for Gaussian training), (d) export splat.ply, (e) manually annotate ground
truth relations by visual inspection of the physical scene.

5.2.3 Challenges

The primary data challenge is the absence of ground truth spatial relation
annotations for real Gaussian splat scenes. ScanNet provides object geometry
but not explicit spatial relation labels — these must be derived from geometry
rules, creating a circular dependency (the GNN is trained on rule-derived
labels and therefore cannot exceed rule accuracy on the same distribution).
The custom scenes have manual annotations but only 8 scenes exist, which is
statistically thin for robust evaluation. The OCTScenes provide additional
volume but annotation is incomplete due to the difficulty of matching HDBSCAN
clusters to physical objects without a cluster visualisation tool.

5.2.4 Cleaning and Preprocessing

Gaussian splats are preprocessed by: (a) filtering out Gaussians with opacity
below 0.1 (removes semi-transparent background), (b) Statistical Outlier
Removal (SOR) with 20 neighbours and 2.0 standard deviation ratio (removes
isolated floater Gaussians that are artifacts of the reconstruction process).
These two steps typically reduce the Gaussian count from 200,000-500,000 to
150,000-350,000 while preserving all object-relevant Gaussians.

5.3 Data Processing
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The ScanNet cache system processes each scene once and stores the resulting
graph as a .pt file. The cache is versioned (currently v7_margins) to ensure
that changes to the feature schema automatically invalidate old cached files.
Processing 1513 ScanNet scenes takes approximately 35-40 minutes on first run
and loads in under 30 seconds on subsequent runs.

For each ScanNet scene, the processing pipeline produces a graph dictionary
containing: node features (N x 10), edge index (2 x E for all directed pairs),
edge attributes (E x 17), multi-hot edge labels (E x 12), object semantic
labels, and scene identifier.

5.4 Model Building
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The relation prediction model evolved through seven major versions:

Version 1-3: Single-head GAT with 8-dim node features and 4-dim edge features,
trained on 3DSSG-derived labels. These early versions used softmax single-label
classification and achieved poor results due to the multi-label nature of
spatial relations (an object can simultaneously be on_top_of, higher_than, and
left_of another object).

Version 4 (axis-aligned): Switched to multi-label BCE loss with per-class
pos_weight. Applied ScanNet axis-alignment matrices to canonicalise scene
orientation, making in_front_of/behind learnable. Expanded to 10-dim node
features and 10-dim edge features with signed directional displacements
(delta_x, delta_y) that encode left/right/front/behind directly.

Version 5 (honest splits): Fixed critical data leakage bug where augmentation
was applied before train/test split, allowing augmented versions of the same
scene to appear in both splits. Added all-pairs negatives (edges with no
relation get all-zero labels) to teach the model that some pairs have no
spatial relation. This reduced reported F1 from inflated values but produced
credible, honest numbers.

Version 6 (contact features): Added 4 contact-specific edge features
(vert_gap normalised by object height, absolute vert_gap, contact_score
composite, support_overlap) to address the finding that scene-extent
normalisation was destroying the contact signal needed for on_top_of/under
prediction.

Version 7 (dual-head, current): The current architecture uses:
  - 2-layer GAT backbone with 4 attention heads and residual connections
  - Dual prediction heads: one for directional relations (left_of, right_of,
    in_front_of, behind, higher_than, lower_than) using only the first 10
    edge features, and one for contact/physical relations (on_top_of, under,
    inside, attached_to, adjacent_to, hanging_from) using all 17 edge features
    plus a dedicated contact feature encoder
  - Asymmetric focal loss (gamma_neg=2.0) for contact relations to handle
    hard negatives
  - Per-relation threshold tuning on validation set
  - Hierarchical constraint enforcement at inference (on_top_of implies
    higher_than, mutual exclusivity of left/right)
  - 3 rule-margin features that encode proximity to the geometry rule decision
    boundaries

The 17-dimensional edge feature vector consists of:
  [0-2]   Signed centroid displacement (delta_x, delta_y, delta_z)
  [3-4]   Unsigned distances (xy_distance, dist_3d)
  [5]     XY bounding box overlap fraction
  [6]     Volume ratio (min/max)
  [7]     Height ratio (log-normalised)
  [8]     Vertical gap (scene-extent normalised)
  [9]     XY size ratio (log-normalised)
  [10]    Vertical gap normalised by mean object height
  [11]    Absolute vertical gap in metres (clipped)
  [12]    Contact score (composite: exp(-|gap|/threshold) * overlap * above)
  [13]    Support overlap (XY intersection / B's footprint)
  [14]    On-top-of Z margin (distance to rule threshold)
  [15]    On-top-of XY margin (distance to footprint boundary)
  [16]    Z dominance margin (Z diff vs XY distance ratio)

Training uses the Adam optimiser with cosine annealing learning rate schedule,
warmup for 5 epochs, batch size 8, and early stopping with patience 10 on
validation macro F1.

5.5 System Architecture
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The complete system architecture consists of the following modules:

  1. Gaussian Loader (src/gaussian/loader.py): Reads .ply files in 3DGS format,
     extracts per-Gaussian position, opacity, SH colour coefficients, and
     covariance parameters. Computes eigenvalues from covariance matrices.

  2. Gaussian Filter: Removes low-opacity Gaussians (threshold 0.1) and
     applies Statistical Outlier Removal to prune isolated floaters.

  3. HDBSCAN Clustering (src/gaussian/clustering.py): Clusters filtered
     Gaussians into objects using position and colour features. An auto-tuning
     mechanism sweeps min_cluster_size and cluster selection methods (eom/leaf)
     to find the configuration closest to an expected object count (when
     available). Outputs Object3D instances with centroid, bounding box, point
     count, mean colour, mean opacity, and covariance eigenvalues.

  4. Feature Extraction: Computes 10-dim node features and 17-dim edge features
     for all directed object pairs, matching the ScanNet training schema.

  5. Relation Prediction: One of four modes:
     - Geometry: Hand-crafted rules with scene-adaptive dynamic thresholds
     - Hybrid: GNN prediction with geometry validation
     - Physical: Per-relation logistic regression on 31-dim features
     - Ensemble: Geometry for directional, physical classifiers for contact

  6. Semantic Labeling (optional): YOLO or Grounding DINO projection from
     rendered camera views to assign object labels. Not required for relation
     prediction but useful for human-readable output.

  7. Evaluation: Hungarian matching of predicted clusters to GT objects,
     translation to named triples, precision/recall/F1 computation.

5.6 Evaluation Protocol
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Following recommendations from the 3D scene graph literature (Wald et al.,
2020; Wu et al., 2021) and external code review, the evaluation protocol
separates clustering quality from relation prediction quality:

Relation-Only Evaluation (Primary Paper Metric):
  - Clustering with n_objects_hint (oracle object count)
  - Hungarian matching with distance rejection threshold
  - Evaluate only GT relations where both subject and object are successfully
    matched to predicted clusters
  - Reports: object coverage, pair coverage, relation F1

End-to-End Evaluation (Deployment Metric):
  - Fully automatic clustering (no hints)
  - All GT relations involving unmatched objects count as FN
  - All predictions involving unmatched clusters count as FP
  - Reports: micro F1, macro F1

Clustering Quality (Diagnostic):
  - Per-scene: GT object count vs predicted count
  - Object recall and precision
  - Mean centroid assignment distance
  - Merge and split rates

Bootstrap 95% confidence intervals are computed by resampling over scenes
(n=1000 bootstrap iterations) to account for the small test set size (8
scenes).

6.1 ScanNet Training Results
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The v7 dual-head model was trained on approximately 700 ScanNet scenes (after
filtering scenes with missing files or insufficient objects) with honest
train/val/test splits (no data leakage, augmentation applied only to training
split, all-pairs negatives included).

Final test set performance (ScanNet, 17-dim features, dual-head + focal loss):

  Relation          Baseline(0.5)  Tuned Threshold  + Constraints
  ----------------------------------------------------------------
  on_top_of         0.721          0.847            0.844
  under             0.695          0.848            0.850
  inside            0.684          0.814            0.814
  attached_to       0.712          0.860            0.860
  hanging_from      0.883          0.950            0.950
  adjacent_to       0.929          0.949            0.949
  left_of           0.963          0.964            0.964
  right_of          0.963          0.964            0.964
  in_front_of       0.768          0.830            0.829
  behind            0.767          0.829            0.829
  higher_than       0.933          0.933            0.933
  lower_than        0.929          0.936            0.936
  ----------------------------------------------------------------

  Micro F1          0.889          0.921            0.921

The progression from v5 (Macro F1 = 0.770) to v7 (Macro F1 = 0.894)
demonstrates that the architectural improvements (dual-head, contact features,
rule-margin features, focal loss, threshold tuning) substantially improved
performance on the difficult contact relations. The per-relation threshold
tuning alone contributed approximately 6.5 percentage points to macro F1.

6.2 Cross-Domain Evaluation (ScanNet Model on Real Tabletop Scenes)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The v7 model trained on ScanNet was evaluated on the 8 custom tabletop scenes
using the relation-only protocol (oracle clustering):

  Method                          Obj Cov  Pair Cov  Micro F1  95% CI
  -------------------------------------------------------------------
  Geometry Rules                  0.97     0.93      0.478     [0.371, 0.537]
  GNN v7 (ScanNet pretrained)     0.97     0.93      0.281     [0.213, 0.338]
  GNN v7 (Tabletop fine-tuned)    0.97     0.93      0.322     [0.233, 0.395]

End-to-end evaluation (fully automatic, no hints):

  Method                          Micro F1  95% CI
  -------------------------------------------------
  Geometry Rules                  0.163     [0.105, 0.218]
  GNN v7 (ScanNet pretrained)     0.088     [0.056, 0.119]
  GNN v7 (Tabletop fine-tuned)    0.096     [0.054, 0.136]

Key finding: The domain gap between ScanNet training (Macro F1 = 0.894) and
tabletop deployment (Micro F1 = 0.281 for pretrained, 0.322 for fine-tuned)
is approximately 0.57-0.61 absolute F1 points. This represents a severe
failure of cross-domain transfer.

6.3 Fine-Tuning Experiment
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

To address the domain gap, 200 synthetic tabletop scene graphs were generated
with physics-aware placement (stacking, clustering, flat arrangement) and
realistic noise injection (centroid jitter, bbox expansion, Z drift, object
merge/drop). The v7 model was fine-tuned on these synthetic scenes with frozen
GAT backbone and trainable prediction heads.

Results: Fine-tuning improved the GNN from 0.281 to 0.322 Micro F1 on real
tabletop scenes (a 15% relative improvement). However, this still falls short
of the geometry rules baseline (0.478). The synthetic training data, while
correctly scaled for tabletop scenes, lacks the specific noise characteristics
of real Gaussian splat clusters (covariance distortion, opacity artifacts,
colour bleeding between adjacent objects).

6.4 Comparative Analysis
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The most significant finding of this project to date is that hand-crafted
geometry rules with dynamic scene-adaptive thresholds (Micro F1 = 0.478)
outperform both the ScanNet-pretrained GNN (0.281) and the fine-tuned GNN
(0.322) on real tabletop scenes. This is despite the GNN achieving 0.894
macro F1 on ScanNet test data.

Per-relation analysis reveals that the GNN excels at left_of/right_of (F1 =
0.76 for fine-tuned) but fails on in_front_of/behind and on_top_of. The
geometry rules achieve consistent performance across directional relations
(left_of/right_of F1 = 0.725) because they operate on scale-invariant
geometric principles (signed centroid displacement) rather than learned
feature distributions.

The GNN's failure on contact relations (on_top_of = 0.121 on tabletop vs
0.847 on ScanNet) indicates that the contact features and rule-margin features,
while effective within the ScanNet distribution, do not generalise to the
different noise characteristics of Gaussian splat clusters.

6.5 Clustering Quality Analysis
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Clustering quality with oracle object count (n_objects_hint):
  - Object recall: 96.9% (31/32 GT objects matched across 8 scenes)
  - Only scene_09 under-segments (finds 3 of 4 objects)
  - Mean centroid assignment distance: varies by scene

Clustering quality without hints (fully automatic):
  - HDBSCAN typically finds 7-8 clusters instead of 4-5
  - Over-segmentation is the dominant failure mode
  - Background noise and table surface fragments create spurious clusters
  - The 3x drop from relation-only F1 (0.478) to end-to-end F1 (0.163) is
    entirely attributable to clustering errors

This confirms that clustering is the primary bottleneck for deployment
performance, not relation prediction.

6.6 SHAP Analysis of Physical Features
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A separate experiment trained per-relation logistic regression classifiers on
31-dimensional feature vectors (10 geometry + 7 physical features per object x
3). SHAP analysis revealed that:

  - support_likelihood (derived from Gaussian covariance planarity and height
    ratio) is the 5th most predictive feature for on_top_of
  - Physical features contribute 3-13% of total prediction signal depending
    on relation type (highest for under at 12.9%, on_top_of at 8.4%)
  - Geometry features (delta_z, bbox_overlap, vert_gap) remain dominant for
    all relations

This validates the hypothesis that Gaussian covariance eigenvalues encode
physically meaningful object properties, but the contribution is modest
compared to basic geometric features.

================================================================================
7. PROBLEMS ENCOUNTERED AND CURRENT LIMITATIONS
================================================================================

This section documents the problems encountered during development, presented
honestly for mentor guidance.

7.1 Data Leakage in Initial Training (Resolved)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The initial ScanNet training applied geometric augmentation (rotations, flips,
jitter) to the full dataset before performing the train/test split. This meant
that augmented versions of the same scene could appear in both training and
test sets, inflating reported F1 scores. This was identified through external
code review and fixed by splitting by scene first, then augmenting only the
training split. The fix reduced reported numbers but produced credible results.

7.2 Circular Label Problem (Partially Resolved)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

ScanNet does not provide explicit spatial relation annotations. Relations are
derived from geometry rules (derive_relations()) applied to ground truth
bounding boxes. This creates a fundamental ceiling: the GNN is trained to
approximate the geometry rules and therefore cannot exceed rule accuracy on
the same distribution. The addition of all-pairs negatives (teaching the model
that some pairs have no relation) partially addresses this by providing signal
that the rules alone do not generate. However, the fundamental circularity
remains — the GNN's labels come from the same rules it is compared against.

7.3 Domain Gap (Unresolved — Primary Challenge)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The most significant unresolved problem is the domain gap between ScanNet
(room-scale, mesh-based, clean geometry) and real tabletop Gaussian splats
(small-scale, noisy clusters, covariance artifacts). Despite normalisation
efforts, contact features, rule-margin features, and synthetic fine-tuning,
the GNN does not outperform geometry rules on real scenes.

Root causes identified:
  - Scale mismatch: ScanNet rooms span 3-8m, tabletop scenes span 0.3-0.8m
  - Noise characteristics differ: ScanNet has clean mesh geometry, Gaussian
    splats have covariance distortion and opacity artifacts
  - The GNN hallucinates ScanNet-specific relations (attached_to, inside)
    that rarely occur in tabletop scenes
  - Tuned thresholds calibrated for ScanNet distributions do not transfer

7.4 Clustering Instability (Unresolved — Deployment Bottleneck)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Without oracle object count guidance, HDBSCAN consistently over-segments
tabletop scenes (finding 7-8 clusters instead of 4-5). This creates spurious
false positives and causes a 3x performance drop from relation-only to
end-to-end evaluation. The auto-tuning mechanism (sweeping min_cluster_size)
helps but does not reliably converge to the correct object count.

Potential solutions under consideration:
  - Post-clustering merge based on colour similarity and spatial adjacency
  - Stability-based cluster rejection (remove unstable clusters)
  - Silhouette score or DBCV for automatic parameter selection
  - Alternative clustering approaches (spectral clustering, learned methods)

7.5 OCTScenes Annotation Blocked
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The 19 OCTScenes have been processed through NerfStudio and annotation
templates generated. However, manual annotation requires identifying which
HDBSCAN cluster corresponds to which physical object in the scene. Without a
cluster visualisation tool (rendering the splat coloured by cluster ID from
the same viewpoint as a reference image), this identification is unreliable.
Building this tool is a prerequisite for completing the annotation.

7.6 Synthetic Training Data Insufficient
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The 200 synthetic tabletop scenes generated with direct feature computation
(no rendering, no real Gaussian noise) improved the GNN by 15% relative but
did not close the gap to geometry rules. The synthetic features are "too clean"
— they lack the specific noise patterns of real Gaussian splat clusters
(covariance distortion from reconstruction errors, opacity bleeding between
adjacent objects, colour artifacts at object boundaries). A full BlenderProc
pipeline (render → NerfStudio → real splat) would produce training data with
matching noise characteristics but requires significant setup and compute time.

7.7 Evaluation with Only 8 Test Scenes
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Eight test scenes provide limited statistical power. Bootstrap confidence
intervals are wide (e.g., geometry rules: 0.478 [0.371, 0.537]). The
difference between geometry rules (0.478) and fine-tuned GNN (0.322) is
statistically significant, but finer distinctions between methods cannot be
reliably made. Completing the OCTScenes annotation would provide 19 additional
test scenes (27 total), substantially improving statistical power.

8. PROPOSED NEXT STEPS (Seeking Mentor Guidance)
================================================================================

The following directions are under consideration. Mentor guidance is sought on
prioritisation and feasibility.

8.1 Fix Clustering (High Priority)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The 3x performance drop from relation-only to end-to-end evaluation indicates
that clustering is the primary deployment bottleneck. Proposed approaches:
  - Implement post-clustering merge (colour + spatial proximity)
  - Add stability-based cluster rejection
  - Investigate alternative clustering methods
  - Build an object count estimator from scene statistics

8.2 Full BlenderProc Synthetic Pipeline (High Priority)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Generate 50-60 synthetic tabletop scenes with the full pipeline: BlenderProc
physics simulation → rendered frames → NerfStudio → real Gaussian splats.
This produces training data with real Gaussian noise characteristics, which
the direct feature generation approach lacks. Expected to close the domain gap
between synthetic training and real deployment.

8.3 Complete OCTScenes Annotation (Medium Priority)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Build the cluster visualisation tool (render splat coloured by cluster ID),
annotate 19 OCTScenes, and use them as additional test data. This would
provide 27 total test scenes and substantially improve evaluation reliability.

8.4 Symbolic Consistency Repair (Medium Priority)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Implement deterministic constraint propagation as a post-processing step:
  - Inverse consistency: on_top_of(A,B) implies under(B,A)
  - Exclusivity: left_of(A,B) conflicts with right_of(A,B)
  - Support logic: on_top_of(A,B) implies higher_than(A,B)
  - Graph repair: remove lower-confidence contradictions

This is a lightweight addition that could improve precision without requiring
additional training data.

8.5 End-User Application 
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Build a Streamlit application that allows users to:
  - Upload a video or select a pre-processed scene
  - View the reconstructed scene graph
  - Query spatial relations in natural language
  - Visualise object clusters and their relationships

This is a mentor requirement for the final deliverable.

8.6 Paper Framing Decision
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Based on current results, two paper framings are possible:

  Option A: "We show that geometry rules are a strong baseline for tabletop
  3DGS scene graphs, and characterise why learned models fail to surpass them
  due to domain gap. We propose Gaussian-aware physical features and symbolic
  consistency repair as complementary improvements."

  Option B: "We close the domain gap through real-splat synthetic training
  (BlenderProc) and demonstrate that a fine-tuned GNN surpasses geometry rules
  on tabletop scenes." (Requires BlenderProc pipeline to succeed.)

Mentor guidance is sought on which framing to pursue.

================================================================================
9. CONCLUSION
================================================================================

This progress report presents the current state of LogicSplat, a
foundation-model-free system for 3D scene graph generation from consumer
smartphone video via Gaussian Splatting. The project has achieved a functional
end-to-end pipeline, a well-trained GNN that achieves Macro F1 = 0.894 on
ScanNet test data, and a rigorous evaluation protocol that separates clustering
quality from relation prediction quality.

The primary finding to date is that the domain gap between room-scale training
data and tabletop deployment remains the central challenge. Geometry rules with
dynamic thresholds (Micro F1 = 0.478) outperform both the pretrained and
fine-tuned GNN on real tabletop scenes. This is an honest result that reflects
the genuine difficulty of cross-domain transfer in 3D scene understanding.

The project has identified two clear bottlenecks: (1) clustering instability
without oracle guidance, and (2) the noise distribution mismatch between
synthetic training features and real Gaussian splat clusters. Addressing these
through improved clustering and real-splat synthetic training (BlenderProc)
represents the most promising path to publication-worthy results.

Mentor feedback is sought on prioritisation of next steps, paper framing, and
whether the current results and honest characterisation of limitations
constitute a viable workshop paper contribution.
